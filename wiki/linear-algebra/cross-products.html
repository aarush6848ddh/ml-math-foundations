Cross Products

From 3Blue1Brown, Essence of Linear Algebra, Chapter 10
Linear Algebra Lesson 10
Concept

Getting a Vector Perpendicular to Two Others

The cross product takes two vectors in 3D space and produces a third vector that is perpendicular to both of them. Unlike the dot product, which collapses two vectors into a single number, the cross product gives you back a vector. That vector points in the direction your right thumb points when your fingers curl from the first vector toward the second.

The core idea: the cross product \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}\) produces a vector perpendicular to both \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\), with length equal to the area of the parallelogram they span.

The 2D version: a signed area

In 2D, the cross product is not a vector but a scalar: the determinant of the matrix whose columns are the two vectors. This gives you the signed area of the parallelogram formed by the two vectors. Positive means the second vector is counterclockwise from the first. Negative means it is clockwise. Zero means the vectors are parallel and span no area at all.

The 3D version: a new vector

In 3D, the cross product is computed using a determinant-style expansion with the standard basis vectors \(\hat{i}\), \(\hat{j}\), \(\hat{k}\) placed in the first row. The result is a vector orthogonal to both inputs. Its length equals the area of the parallelogram spanned by the two input vectors, which means it gets larger when the inputs are more perpendicular and shrinks toward zero when they point in the same direction.

Right-hand rule and orientation

The direction of the cross product follows the right-hand rule. Point your fingers along \(\mathbf{v}\), curl them toward \(\mathbf{w}\), and your thumb points in the direction of \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}\). This makes the cross product sensitive to order: \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w} = -(\mathbf{w} \times \mathbf{v})\).

Formulas

Key Formulas

2D cross product (signed area)
\[ \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w} = \det\begin{bmatrix} v_1 & w_1 \\ v_2 & w_2 \end{bmatrix} = v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1 \]
3D cross product
\[ \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} v_2 w_3 - v_3 w_2 \\ v_3 w_1 - v_1 w_3 \\ v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1 \end{bmatrix} \]
Length equals parallelogram area
\[ \|\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\| \, \|\mathbf{w}\| \sin\theta \]
Anti-commutativity
\[ \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w} = -(\mathbf{w} \times \mathbf{v}) \]
Code

Code Examples

All code below is taken verbatim from lesson-10-cross-products.py.

Part 1: 2D cross product

In 2D, the cross product is the determinant of the matrix formed by stacking the two vectors as columns. The result is the signed area of their parallelogram.

Python lesson-10-cross-products.py 
import numpy as np

v = np.array([3, 1])
w = np.array([1, 2])

# build the matrix with v as column 1 and w as column 2
matrix = np.column_stack((v, w))

# the cross product is the determinant
cross = np.linalg.det(matrix)
print(f"2D cross product: {cross}")
print(f"Area of parallelogram: {abs(cross)}")

# if positive, w is counterclockwise from v
# if negative, w is clockwise from v
Output
2D cross product: 5.000000000000001
Area of parallelogram: 5.000000000000001

Part 2: 3D cross product

In 3D, np.cross computes the cross product vector directly. We verify it is perpendicular to both inputs by checking that both dot products are zero.

Python lesson-10-cross-products.py 
v = np.array([3, 1, 0])
w = np.array([1, 2, 7])

# compute cross product
cross = np.cross(v, w)

print(f"Resulting Vector: {cross}")

# length of the cross product vector = area of the parallelogram
print(f"Length (area): {np.linalg.norm(cross)}")

# verify it is perpendicular to both v and w
# dot product with either should be zero
print(f"Perpendicular to v: {np.isclose(np.dot(cross, v), 0)}")
print(f"Perpendicular to w: {np.isclose(np.dot(cross, w), 0)}")
Output
Resulting Vector: [  7 -21   5]
Length (area): 22.693611435820433
Perpendicular to v: True
Perpendicular to w: True

Part 3: Properties

Perpendicular vectors produce the largest cross product. Nearly parallel vectors produce almost nothing. Scaling one vector by a factor scales the cross product by the same factor.

Python lesson-10-cross-products.py 
# perpendicular vectors have larger cross product
v_perp1 = np.array([1, 0, 0])
v_perp2 = np.array([0, 1, 0])

# similar direction vectors have smaller cross product
v_sim1 = np.array([1, 0, 0])
v_sim2 = np.array([1, 0.1, 0])

print(f"Perpendicular cross product length: {np.linalg.norm(np.cross(v_perp1, v_perp2))}")
print(f"Similar direction cross product length: {np.linalg.norm(np.cross(v_sim1, v_sim2))}")

# scaling one vector scales the cross product by the same factor
v = np.array([1, 0, 0])
w = np.array([0, 1, 0])

original = np.linalg.norm(np.cross(v, w))
scaled   = np.linalg.norm(np.cross(3 * v, w))

print(f"Original cross product length: {original}")
print(f"Scaled by 3 cross product length: {scaled}")
print(f"Is scaled version 3x original? {np.isclose(scaled, 3 * original)}")
Output
Perpendicular cross product length: 1.0
Similar direction cross product length: 0.09999999999999999
Original cross product length: 1.0
Scaled by 3 cross product length: 3.0
Is scaled version 3x original? True
AI Connection

How This Connects to AI

Why this matters for machine learning

The cross product is less directly used in AI than the dot product, but the intuitions behind it show up in important places. The key idea is that the cross product measures how perpendicular two vectors are. Parallel vectors produce a cross product of zero. Perpendicular vectors produce the maximum. This is the same intuition behind why orthogonal weight matrices are useful in deep learning.

Orthogonal matrices have columns that are all mutually perpendicular unit vectors. When you multiply by an orthogonal matrix, you rotate the space without stretching or compressing anything. This preserves vector lengths and the angles between them. During backpropagation, this means gradients flow cleanly without exploding or vanishing, which is a serious problem in deep networks. Techniques like orthogonal initialization and orthogonal regularization use this property to stabilize training.

The right-hand rule and orientation concepts from the cross product show up directly in 3D deep learning. Models like PointNet and its successors process raw point clouds for tasks like 3D object detection in self-driving cars. These models operate on sets of 3D coordinates and need to reason about surface normals, orientations, and spatial relationships. Surface normals are literally cross products: given two edges of a mesh triangle, the normal is their cross product. Understanding orientation in 3D space is fundamental to these systems.

More broadly, the idea that two perpendicular things produce zero and two aligned things produce maximum runs throughout all of linear algebra. It is why orthogonal bases are so useful for decomposing signals, why PCA finds directions that are perpendicular to each other, and why attention heads in transformers can specialize without interfering with each other when their weight matrices are approximately orthogonal.
