Change of Basis

From 3Blue1Brown, Essence of Linear Algebra, Chapter 13
Linear Algebra Lesson 12
Concept

Every Coordinate System Is Just a Choice

When you write a vector as \([3, 7]\), you are implicitly saying "3 steps along the x-axis and 7 steps along the y-axis." But that is just one choice of coordinate system. Someone else could describe the exact same point in space using completely different basis vectors, and they would write different numbers. Change of basis is the tool for translating between those descriptions.

The core idea: if someone else's basis vectors are the columns of matrix \(B\), then multiplying by \(B\) converts their coordinates into yours. Multiplying by \(B^{-1}\) converts your coordinates into theirs.

The change of basis matrix

You build the change of basis matrix by stacking the alternative basis vectors as columns. If Jennifer uses basis vectors \(\mathbf{b}_1\) and \(\mathbf{b}_2\), then \(B = [\mathbf{b}_1 \mid \mathbf{b}_2]\). When you compute \(B\mathbf{v}\), you are asking: if Jennifer says the vector is \(\mathbf{v}\), where does that actually land in our coordinate system? The columns of \(B\) tell you where Jennifer's basis vectors live in your world, so matrix-vector multiplication does the conversion automatically.

Going the other way

To go from your coordinates into Jennifer's, you use the inverse: \(B^{-1}\mathbf{v}\). The inverse undoes the change of basis. It asks: what combination of Jennifer's basis vectors would produce this vector?

Translating a transformation

If you have a transformation matrix \(M\) written in your coordinate system, and you want to express that same transformation in Jennifer's system, the formula is \(B^{-1} M B\). This pattern appears constantly in linear algebra. You first translate into your system (\(B\)), apply the transformation (\(M\)), then translate back (\(B^{-1}\)). The result is a matrix that does the same geometric thing but is expressed in Jennifer's language.

Formulas

Key Formulas

Change of basis matrix
\[ B = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{b}_1 & \mathbf{b}_2 \end{bmatrix} \]
Convert their coords to ours
\[ \mathbf{v}_\text{ours} = B \, \mathbf{v}_\text{theirs} \]
Convert our coords to theirs
\[ \mathbf{v}_\text{theirs} = B^{-1} \mathbf{v}_\text{ours} \]
Transformation in their coordinate system
\[ M_\text{theirs} = B^{-1} M B \]
Code

Code Examples

All code below is taken verbatim from lesson-12-change-of-basis.py.

Part 1: Change of basis matrix

Jennifer's basis vectors become the columns of the change of basis matrix. Multiplying by it converts her coordinates into ours.

Python lesson-12-change-of-basis.py 
import numpy as np

# Jennifer's basis vectors in our coordinate system
b1 = np.array([2, 1])
b2 = np.array([-1, 1])

# change of basis matrix — columns are Jennifer's basis vectors
change_of_basis = np.column_stack((b1, b2))

# Jennifer describes a vector with these coordinates in her system
jennifer_coords = np.array([1, 6])

# convert to our coordinate system
our_coords = change_of_basis @ jennifer_coords
print(f"Jennifer's coords: {jennifer_coords}")
print(f"Our coords: {our_coords}")
Output
Jennifer's coords: [1 6]
Our coords: [-4  7]

Part 2: Going the other way

The inverse of the change of basis matrix converts our coordinates into Jennifer's system.

Python lesson-12-change-of-basis.py 
# a vector in our system
our_vector = np.array([3, 7])

# convert to Jennifer's system using the inverse
inverse_cob = np.linalg.inv(change_of_basis)
jennifer_vector = inverse_cob @ our_vector
print(f"Our vector: {our_vector}")
print(f"Jennifer's vector: {jennifer_vector}")
Output
Our vector: [3 7]
Jennifer's vector: [3.33333333 3.66666667]

Part 3: Translating a transformation

To express a transformation in Jennifer's coordinate system, use the formula \(B^{-1} M B\). The result is a matrix that describes the same geometric action but written in her language.

Python lesson-12-change-of-basis.py 
# a reflection across the y-axis in our system
rotation = np.array([
    [-1, 0],
    [0,  1]
])

# express the transformation in Jennifer's coordinate system
# formula: inv(change_of_basis) @ rotation @ change_of_basis
jennifer_rotation = np.linalg.inv(change_of_basis) @ rotation @ change_of_basis
print(f"Rotation in our system: {rotation}")
print(f"Rotation in Jennifer's system: {jennifer_rotation}")
Output
Rotation in our system: [[-1  0]
 [ 0  1]]
Rotation in Jennifer's system: [[-0.33333333  0.66666667]
 [ 1.33333333  0.33333333]]
AI Connection

How This Connects to AI

Why this matters for machine learning

Change of basis is exactly what happens inside transformer attention. When attention computes the query, key, and value matrices, it is projecting the input token embeddings into three different coordinate systems simultaneously. The weight matrices \(W_Q\), \(W_K\), and \(W_V\) are change of basis operations. They take the raw embedding and re-express it in a coordinate system where similarity comparisons are more meaningful.

Multi-head attention takes this further. Each attention head uses its own set of projection matrices, which means each head operates in its own coordinate system. One head might project into a subspace where grammatical relationships are easiest to see. Another might find a subspace where semantic similarity is clearest. The model learns these basis changes during training, discovering which coordinate systems make patterns in language most visible.

The formula \(B^{-1} M B\) also appears in how researchers analyze what transformers have learned. If you want to understand a weight matrix \(M\) in a more interpretable coordinate system, you can apply a change of basis to rotate it into a frame where its structure is clearer. This is the idea behind techniques like probing classifiers and representation analysis in interpretability research.

More broadly, any time a neural network applies a linear layer, it is changing the coordinate system of its input. The entire forward pass of a transformer is a sequence of coordinate changes, each learned to make the next operation easier. Understanding change of basis gives you the geometric intuition for why stacking linear layers with nonlinearities between them is such a powerful way to process information.
